## Part A — the direct and close-adjacent competitors

### A1. Structurely (AI ISA / conversational lead follow-up, real estate)

**What it is:** "Conversational AI Platform for Voice, SMS & Email" — its AI persona
("Aisa Holmes") texts and emails new leads within seconds of form-fill, asks qualifying
questions, and — the closest single feature to FollowUp's own thesis — **keeps following
up on a lead for 12+ months** until it responds or converts.
[structurely.com](https://www.structurely.com/), accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**The hook:** Not a punchy headline formula so much as a *mechanism* claim stated plainly:
instant two-way conversation the moment a lead fills a form, sustained for months. The
pitch is speed + persistence, not a clever phrase. Pricing has moved to **$299/mo** entry
(up from $179/mo as of the 2026-09-06 pass — see
`followup/research/market/2026-09-08-realtor-competitor-refresh.md`), positioned around a
real-estate-specific ROI story (cost of one closed deal vastly exceeds the monthly fee),
not a flat "everything included" number.

**What FollowUp should learn:** the "keeps trying until it responds" claim is a strong,
concrete promise — much stronger than a vague "AI follow-up." FollowUp's own version of
this claim (rescue score, the neglect trigger, "About to be lost") is real and could be
stated with the same directness.

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** Structurely is vertical-locked (real estate ISA
persona, "Aisa Holmes" branding) and now backed by a holding company rather than an
independent product — its trust story leans on "9-year track record, 13M+ conversations"
scale-proof, which FollowUp doesn't have yet and shouldn't fake.

### A2. Ylopo (AI lead gen + nurture, real estate)

**What it is:** AI-driven digital marketing *and* lead-generation platform for real estate
agents, with an AI texting/voice assistant ("Raiya," formerly "rAIya") that nurtures new
leads and **reactivates cold ones the team had given up on**.
[ylopo.com](https://www.ylopo.com/), accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**The hook:** Ylopo's own copy leans on the word "reactivating" for its cold-lead feature
— language striking close to FollowUp's own "rescue" framing, but bundled inside a paid
lead-*generation* product (ad spend + platform fee), not sold as a standalone conversion
tool. All-in cost for a working setup runs **$1,500–3,500+/mo** once ad spend and the AI
add-on are included (Raiya alone is $500–795/mo) — see
`followup/research/market/2026-09-08-realtor-competitor-refresh.md` for the breakdown.

**What FollowUp should learn:** Ylopo's own website copy validates the "cold lead
reactivation" concept as a sellable, named feature — evidence a market exists for exactly
FollowUp's Point 2 (rescue). But note the framing risk directly: Ylopo pitches this
*inside* a lead-generation platform, which is the exact positioning FollowUp's thesis
explicitly rejects ("we don't get you more leads").

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** bundling generation and conversion into one undifferentiated
pitch. FollowUp's differentiation is sharper precisely because it refuses to also sell the
generation half.

### A3. Artisan AI (artisan.co) — AI SDR, adjacent category, cautionary tale

**What it is:** "Ava," an autonomous AI BDR — finds leads, researches them, writes
personalized outbound, books meetings. Different motion from FollowUp (outbound
prospecting to strangers, not inbound follow-up to an existing lead) but the most
aggressive AI-personality marketing found in this pass.
[artisan.co](https://www.artisan.co/), accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**The hook:** "Hire Ava, the AI BDR that books meetings on autopilot" (**paraphrase of
CTA copy, close to verbatim per search snippet**) — the company frames its AI as a
literal employee ("Artisans"), and ran a widely covered, controversial outdoor ad
campaign literally titled **"Stop Hiring Humans."**
Sources: [Artisan Series A post](https://www.artisan.co/blog/artisan-series-a), [SF
Standard on the ad campaign, 2025-04-07](https://sfstandard.com/2025/04/07/the-real-person-behind-san-franciscos-hated-anti-human-ad-campaign/).

**What is interesting:** the campaign got real press — but as controversy, not
admiration. The SF Standard's own headline calls it "hated." This is a naturally occurring
experiment in the exact anti-pattern FollowUp's brand principles already rule out (AI as
personality, not mechanism; the product replacing a person as the *pitch* rather than
a quiet outcome).

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** anthropomorphizing the AI ("hire your AI employee"),
using job-replacement as the emotional hook, or any campaign that reads as gloating about
automation replacing a human. FollowUp's own mission literally includes "remove the
position of the salesperson" — the *outcome* is similar, but Artisan's *marketing voice*
about that outcome is close to the worst way to say it out loud to the actual buyer (a
small business owner, who is often that same person). This is a direct, named collision
with brand principle 3 ("AI is invisible capability, never personality") and worth stating
as a hard boundary, not just a stylistic note.

### A4. Podium — missed-call/lead-response, closest business-model peer

**What it is:** originally a review/messaging platform, now repositioning itself. Its own
page title as indexed in 2026 reads **"Podium: AI Lead Generation & Lead Management
Platform"** — Podium has moved toward "lead generation" language, not away from it.
[podium.com](https://www.podium.com/), accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**The hook (verbatim, per search snippet):** *"Slow response times, disconnected tools,
and missed follow-up cost you revenue. Podium helps you respond faster, convert more
demand, and run more efficiently."* A testimonial sits directly in or near the hero:
*"Within a month of adding Podium, revenue jumped 12% because we never miss a call."*

**Pattern:** problem-cost-first, then a three-part promise (respond faster / convert more
/ run more efficiently), then a proof-point testimonial with a specific number
immediately after the promise — not buried in a later section.

**Pricing:** **fully hidden.** Podium's pricing page shows no figures at all — it asks for
your industry, then offers a "Get custom quote" button. Reviewers and reference sites are
blunt about this: *"Its pricing page carries no figures at all."* Complaints cluster
around undisclosed add-on fees (extra users, extra phone numbers, a 10DLC compliance fee,
AI feature add-ons) that only surface once you're in a contract, plus reported difficulty
cancelling.
Sources: [Podium pricing page](https://www.podium.com/getpricing), [Quo/OpenPhone pricing
breakdown](https://www.quo.com/blog/podium-pricing/), accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**What FollowUp should learn:** the hook formula (cost of the problem stated in money
terms → three-part promise → an immediate specific-number proof point) is genuinely
effective and worth the *pattern*, not the words. The pricing opacity is the single
clearest "what a weaker page does that FollowUp should never do" — flat $29/mo, shown
plainly, is a structural trust advantage against exactly this pattern.

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** the drift toward "lead generation" language despite
Podium's actual mechanism being response/conversion — this is the same positioning
confusion FollowUp's own thesis is built to avoid. Also: hidden pricing, "custom quote,"
and industry-selector gates before showing any number.

### A5. Housecall Pro — home-services vertical SaaS, trust-by-scale

**What it is:** field-service management (scheduling, invoicing, follow-ups) for home
service pros.
[housecallpro.com](https://www.housecallpro.com/), accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**The hook:** leads with scale, not a clever line: **"200K+ Pros trust Housecall Pro to
run scheduling, invoicing and follow-ups automatically"** (near-verbatim per search
snippet). The number is the headline; the specific feature list is secondary copy under
it.

**What FollowUp should learn:** for a category where the buyer is skeptical of software
in general (a contractor, not a software person — closely matching FollowUp's own ICP),
"a very large number of people like me already trust this" can out-perform a clever
headline. It substitutes for FollowUp's harder problem: FollowUp doesn't have 200K
customers yet, so it cannot use this pattern honestly today — worth flagging as a "when
we have the number, lead with it" note rather than a current recommendation.

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** stating a customer count that isn't real or rounding
generously — S-01/S-15 territory (scammy, template-feeling) if the number isn't durable
and accurate.

## Part B — CRM/lead-management incumbents

### B1. HubSpot

**What it is:** the category's largest, broadest platform — CRM, marketing, sales,
service, and (as of 2026) an "agentic customer platform" built around autonomous "Breeze
Agents."
[hubspot.com](https://www.hubspot.com/), [CMSWire on Spring 2026
Spotlight](https://www.cmswire.com/digital-experience/hubspot-launches-aeo-expands-ai-agents-at-spring-2026-spotlight/),
accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**The hook, 2026:** the company's own positioning has shifted its center of gravity
to **"agentic customer platform"** — bringing customer/business data into one place so
"human and AI employees" can work together. This is the platform-of-everything pitch:
broad, capability-listing, not a single sharp promise.

**The dashboard-relevant finding worth restating here** (already surfaced in the CEO's
brief, re-confirmed this pass): HubSpot's own internal guidance for its sales workspace
states plainly — *"each account should arrive with a reason it deserves attention: a
research-intent signal, a meaningful return visit, a high-fit account that has gone
untouched, or a score crossing a validated threshold,"* and *"a rep opening HubSpot
should see a prioritized queue — not an alphabetical contact list or 47 overdue
tasks."* [HubSpot Knowledge Base, "review sales activity in the sales
workspace"](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/prospecting/review-sales-activity-in-the-sales-workspace),
accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13. This is the single strongest validation found in this
whole pass that FollowUp's "score with a visible reason" design instinct is right, coming
from the category leader's own product documentation, not a competitor's marketing claim.

**Complaint pattern (G2/reviews, aggregated across several review-summary sources):**
*"the interface and options can be very overwhelming and confusing"*; the platform *"tries
to be everything to everyone"*; users report *"spending significant time learning where
features live."* One comparison notes HubSpot's dashboards are less flexible than a
dedicated BI tool for anyone who wants to drill into data. Sources:
[learn.g2.com "Is HubSpot Worth It?"](https://learn.g2.com/is-hubspot-worth-it),
[G2 HubSpot Sales Hub reviews](https://www.g2.com/products/hubspot-sales-hub/reviews),
accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13. *Graded C — these are secondary summaries of G2
sentiment, not a raw pull of individual reviews; consistent with the "bloat" finding
already on file in `followup/PRODUCT_DIRECTION.md`'s reference to prior research.*

**What FollowUp should learn:** HubSpot's own written design philosophy for its "prioritized
queue, not an alphabetical list" is exactly FollowUp's dashboard job description. It's
safe, and useful, to treat this as external validation from the category leader rather
than re-deriving it.

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** the platform-of-everything marketing voice, or the
resulting complexity/discoverability complaints. HubSpot's dashboard *design intent*
(prioritized queue) is right; HubSpot's *actual shipped breadth* is the thing every
weaker competitor gets punished for imitating.

### B2. Close

**What it is:** "the CRM built for action" (**paraphrase, close to verbatim** per search
result) — calling, email, SMS, tasks, and pipeline in one tool, aimed at small/mid sales
teams who need speed, not enterprise configurability.
[close.com/crm](https://close.com/crm), accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**Pricing, verbatim structure:** four tiers, **Solo $9 → Essentials $35 → Growth $99
→ Scale $139** per user/month, billed annually — the entry price ("from $9/user") is
real but capped (single user, 10,000-lead limit), a classic anchor-low pattern. Source:
[MarketBetter Close CRM Pricing 2026](https://marketbetter.ai/blog/close-crm-pricing-2026/),
accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13; cross-checked against Close's own
[pricing page](https://close.com/pricing) listing in search results. *Graded C —
third-party pricing aggregator, not Close's page fetched directly, but the tier
structure and price points are consistent across multiple independent sources checked.*

**The feature worth naming here is "Smart Views" and the concept of "lead rot":** Close's
own help documentation uses the phrase **"Avoiding Lead Rot"** as a section header, and
recommends a saved view for any lead sitting in "Interested" status for more than 10
days specifically **to catch it before it rots**.
[Close Help Center, "Avoiding Lead Rot and Consolidating Smart
Views"](https://help.close.com/docs/smart-view-tips-and-suggestions), accessed via
WebSearch 2026-09-13. Close also markets this directly: *"Smart Views tell sales reps
who to follow up with and when to follow up without the need to create endless tasks and
reminders."*
[Close blog, "How to Catch Missed Sales Leads with Smart Views and
Automation"](https://close.com/blog/crm-automation-prevent-lost-leads), accessed via
WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**What FollowUp should learn:** "lead rot" as a named, documented problem inside a real,
funded CRM's own help center is strong validating language — a category leader has
already built (a manual, saved-search version of) the exact mechanism FollowUp automates.
The word "rot" itself is vivid and worth studying as a category term, even though
FollowUp shouldn't necessarily adopt the same word (see the synthesis file — "lead
rescue" is already crowded, see
`followup/research/market/2026-09-08-broader-competitive-landscape.md`).

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** Close's version of this is a *manual saved filter a
rep has to remember to check* — it names the problem well but doesn't solve it
automatically. That gap (naming rot vs. actually rescuing from it) is exactly FollowUp's
opening.

### B3. Pipedrive

**What it is:** pipeline-first CRM aimed at small sales teams.
[pipedrive.com](https://www.pipedrive.com/), accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.

**What's confirmed:** CTA pattern pairs "Start free" with "No credit card required" and
"Full access" directly adjacent — addressing three objections (cost, commitment, feature
access) in one visual cluster rather than spread across the page.
[Capterra Pipedrive reviews reference](https://www.capterra.com/p/132666/Pipedrive/reviews/?page=51),
accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13. *No exact headline text surfaced this pass — flagged as
an open question rather than guessed at.*

**What FollowUp should learn:** stacking objection-killers directly beside the CTA button
(not in a separate FAQ section) removes friction at the exact moment someone is deciding.
FollowUp's own CTA could do the same (e.g., "no credit card," "cancel anytime," "$29/mo,
everything included" clustered at the button, not just in a pricing section below).

## Part C — broader best-in-class B2B SaaS (for hook/structure principles, not category proof)

### C1. Linear

**What it is:** project/issue tracking for product teams — repeatedly cited across
design-focused sources as *the* current reference point for restrained B2B SaaS design.

**The hook and structure (from a design-analysis roundup, Vezadigital/Userpilot-class
sources, cross-checked against Linear's own changelog voice):** *"The whole page is a
continuous walkthrough of the real product UI in dark mode with no abstract illustrations
or stock dashboard mockups — only actual Linear screens with real-looking issue titles and
live activity."* [Search-aggregated design commentary, accessed via WebSearch
2026-09-13 — paraphrase of multiple sources describing the same page, not a single
verbatim quote.] Homepage tagline (verbatim, per multiple corroborating snippets):
**"Purpose-built for planning and building products. Designed for the AI era."**

**Pricing presentation:** four flat tiers (Free / Basic $10 / Business $16 / Enterprise
custom), no usage metering, no per-feature toggles inside a tier — *"no tracked-user
tiers, no session limits, and no usage-based surprises."* The free tier is generous enough
to actually run a small team's real workflow, not a crippled trial.

**What FollowUp should learn:** showing the *real product*, not an illustration, as the
hero visual is the single most consistent theme across every "best landing pages of 2026"
source found this pass (repeated independently for Linear, Vercel, and the broader trend
commentary — see C2 below). FollowUp's `HeroMockup` component (already in
`followup/src/app/page.tsx`) doing this is the right instinct if it renders honestly, not
an idealized fake screenshot.

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** dark-mode-only aesthetic (FollowUp's brand is warm,
light, calm — a different register, correctly) or Linear's target user's tolerance for
density (engineers living in the tool all day vs. FollowUp's owner on a phone for 90
seconds — brand principle 4 already accounts for this).

### C2. Vercel

**The hook (verbatim):** **"Develop. Preview. Ship."** — three words, three verbs, in
strict sequence, describing the actual product workflow rather than a benefit claim.
[Confirmed across Vercel's own GitHub repo description and multiple mirrors, accessed
via WebSearch 2026-09-13.]

**Pattern:** compress the entire value proposition into a small number of concrete verbs
that map directly onto the user's actual workflow, then let the product visual (a real
deployment pipeline / live preview) demonstrate each verb rather than explain it in prose.

**What FollowUp should learn:** the "verb sequence" headline formula is a genuine,
reusable pattern distinct from "problem → solution" or "contrast" formulas — worth
naming explicitly in the synthesis.

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** this only works when the product's workflow really is
three crisp, sequential, demoable steps. FollowUp's actual mechanism (capture → score →
draft → send-or-hold) is four steps and less visually demoable in three words — forcing
it into this formula would be a worse fit than developing FollowUp's own contrast-based
formula (see the synthesis file).

### C3. Stripe

**The hook:** **"Financial Infrastructure to Grow Your Revenue"** (current, per search
snippet 2026-09-13) — a shift from Stripe's older, more famous "Financial infrastructure
for the internet," now made outcome-oriented ("to grow your revenue") rather than purely
descriptive.

**What is interesting:** even a company as established as Stripe keeps its headline in
motion, and the direction of travel is *away* from purely descriptive ("infrastructure
for the internet") toward stated business outcome ("grow your revenue"). This matters for
FollowUp because "infrastructure" language (what the product *is*) reads as an engineer's
frame; "outcome" language (what changes for the business) reads as an owner's frame — and
FollowUp's buyer is the owner, not an engineer.

**What FollowUp should learn:** describing the mechanism ("we read every channel and
score every lead") is less persuasive on its own than stating the business outcome
("...so a lead doesn't die from silence"). FollowUp's current hero copy already leans
outcome-first ("FollowUp catches the one that went quiet") — this is a validating data
point for staying on that side of the line, not a reason to change it.

**What FollowUp should NOT copy:** Stripe's category (payments infra) has a technical
buyer alongside the business buyer; FollowUp's landing page has only the owner. Don't
borrow Stripe's occasional technical/API-forward sections — irrelevant to FollowUp's
single audience.

## Cross-cutting synthesis — patterns that repeated across three or more examples

### The hook: four repeatable formulas found, not "it's just good copy"

1. **Contrast formula** — "everyone does X, we do Y" (FollowUp's own current headline:
   *"Every tool answers the lead. FollowUp catches the one that went quiet."*). Not found
   verbatim at this exact structure elsewhere in this pass, but it's the same shape as
   Podium's "Slow response... Podium helps you respond faster" (problem stated, then the
   product as the fix) — a contrast dressed as a before/after rather than an "everyone
   vs. us."
2. **Verb-sequence formula** — Vercel's "Develop. Preview. Ship." Works only when the
   product is a small number of crisp, sequential, demoable steps.
3. **Cost-of-problem-then-three-part-promise formula** — Podium: state the dollar cost of
   the problem, then three short promises, then an immediate specific-number proof point.
4. **Scale-as-headline formula** — Housecall Pro: lead with the number of trusting
   customers instead of a clever line. Only honest to use once the number is real and
   large.

**Not found as a winning pattern anywhere in this pass:** a question-headline
("Are you losing leads?"), a fear-first headline with no product visual immediately
after, or a headline naming "AI" as the subject rather than the outcome. Every strong
example either named the outcome (Stripe, Podium) or showed the mechanism directly
(Linear, Vercel) — AI was never the headline's subject anywhere in this research pass,
even in the AI-native companies (Structurely, Artisan, HubSpot's "Breeze Agents" branding
sits in a secondary explainer, not the H1).

### Content order that repeated

Across every example where order could be inferred: **problem or outcome stated in one
line → real product visual (not illustration) → the mechanism explained in plain terms →
a proof point (specific number, named review, or a "trusted by" scale claim) → pricing
positioned as simple/predictable → CTA.** The proof point consistently sits directly
*before* pricing or the final CTA, not scattered earlier — it's the last thing that
removes doubt right before the ask.

### Social proof: what kind, how much, where

- **A single, specific, numeric proof point** (Podium's "revenue jumped 12%," Housecall
  Pro's "200K+ Pros") beat generic testimonial walls in every example found. None of the
  strong examples in this pass showed a testimonial *carousel* as a major page section —
  it was consistently one or two named, numeric claims placed near the CTA, not a wall of
  quote cards.
- **Named customers/logos** appear (implied across sources, not independently verified
  per logo this pass) but function as a background trust signal, not a headline claim.
- Nobody in this pass used a fake or vague testimonial ("Amazing tool! — J.D.") as a
  primary proof point — consistent with the design-brain's existing anti-pattern list
  ("testimonials with no name or company").

### Pricing presentation: how the strong ones make price not feel like a cost

- **Flat and simple beats tiered-and-complex for trust, even when tiered is objectively
  cheaper at entry.** Close's real entry price is $9/user, cheaper than FollowUp's flat
  $29 — but it requires the buyer to model out seats, add-ons, and which tier unlocks
  automation before they can know their real cost. Basecamp's own pricing history is the
  clearest illustration: it moved *away* from tiers to one flat $99/mo (later $299)
  specifically because customers wanted "simple, fair, and predictable" over a cheaper
  theoretical entry price.
  [getmonetizely.com, "Flat Pricing Gone Wrong: The Story of Basecamp's $99 Unlimited
  Plan"](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/flat-pricing-gone-wrong-the-story-of-basecamps-99-unlimited-plan),
  accessed via WebSearch 2026-09-13.
- **The worst pattern found is Podium's fully hidden pricing** — no number at all, an
  industry selector, then "get a custom quote." Every secondary source describing this
  treats it as a genuine trust cost, not a neutral sales-qualification step.
- **The best-regarded pattern is a number stated plainly with what's included directly
  beside it**, not behind a click. None of the flat-pricing examples in this pass hid the
  number behind an email-gate or a "book a call."

### What's conspicuously absent on the strong pages, present on weak ones

- No countdown timers, no "X spots left," no artificial scarcity anywhere in this pass —
  consistent with the design-brain's existing anti-pattern note, now with real
  competitive evidence behind it (Podium and Structurely, the two closest competitors,
  don't use it either — the tactic isn't even common in this specific niche, not just
  ruled out on principle).
- No AI-as-mascot or AI-as-employee framing on the pages regarded well in commentary
  (Linear, Vercel, Stripe). It shows up only on the page that got called out negatively in
  the press for it (Artisan).
- No hidden pricing on any page treated as a positive example; hidden pricing only
  appeared on Podium, which is also the page with the worst-documented trust complaints
  found in this pass (contract lock-in, billing disputes, cancellation difficulty).
- Long feature-grid sections ("50 reasons to choose us") did not appear on any of the
  best-regarded pages in this pass — HubSpot (the platform-of-everything page) is the one
  example leaning toward exhaustive capability-listing, and it's also the one page
  carrying a documented "overwhelming" complaint pattern.
