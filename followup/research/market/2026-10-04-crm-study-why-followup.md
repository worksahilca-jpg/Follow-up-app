# The CRM study: what they do, why FollowUp exists, and whether it is worth it — 2026-10-04

**Why this exists:** the founder, after shipping three simplified screens in a day: *"I am fully demotivated
… I want to deep dive and do the proper research about this product."* Eight questions, answered in order,
with the evidence graded. This pass builds on the nine market files already on file (2026-09-05 to 09-11) and
adds fresh searches on the CRMs he named (GoHighLevel, Leap) and the ones that matter beside them. WebFetch is
blocked for most vendor sites, so outside claims are search snippets: **grade A** = a primary source's own
number, **B** = a company's own page or help centre via snippet, **C** = a review site or blog. Nothing is
invented; where no number exists, it says so.

---

## In short: the eight answers

1. **How CRMs work:** a contact list, a pipeline of stages, a log of activities, and automations that fire
   when a human moves things. The human is the engine. That is also why they fail: the data never gets
   entered.
2. **What they do:** the big ones sell seats to teams and bundle marketing, websites, invoicing and
   dispatch. For a solo owner every one of them is more product than job.
3. **Why we are making this:** owners answer leads in days or never (median 42 hours across 2,241 firms),
   and answering within minutes multiplies the odds of winning the customer. Nobody does the following up
   for the owner who has no staff; the CRMs assume a person will.
4. **Is it valuable?** The problem is real and measured, and owners already pay $400 to $1,000 a month for
   tools that half-solve it. But the *first reply* is being given away (Meta free, Jobber $29, HubSpot free
   drafting). The value left is the follow-through: the second, third and fifth message, across channels,
   with the owner in control. That is narrower than "AI that replies", and more defensible.
5. **Scalable?** The economics are: 76 to 89 percent gross margin, breakeven at about 6 customers with no
   salary and about 130 with a modest one. What does not scale yet is proof: four outside testers, one
   active. Scale is a distribution question, not a cost question.
6. **Competitors:** yes, on every side. Generalist CRMs (HubSpot, Pipedrive, Zoho), all-in-ones for agencies
   (GoHighLevel), vertical suites (Follow Up Boss, Leap, Jobber, Housecall Pro), messaging suites (Podium),
   AI follow-up specialists (Structurely, Ylopo, Verse), and funded horizontal AI receptionists (Beside).
   None is cheap, simple, cross-channel, owner-controlled and follow-through-first at once.
7. **Why buy FollowUp:** today, honestly: it works from the inbox the owner already has, with nothing to
   fill in; it writes in their voice and waits for their OK; it stops the moment the customer answers; it
   does it in any language; it costs a tenth of the alternatives with no contract. Not yet: the proof number
   ("this many customers answered because of FollowUp") that would make the case for us.
8. **How to make it work:** ten owners who open it every day before one more feature. Pick the shortest
   path to them even while the product stays horizontal. Prove one number per customer. Price above $29.
   Don't fight for the first reply; own the follow-through and the record.

## 1. How CRMs usually work (the high-level model)

Every CRM, from Salesforce to a $14-a-seat tool, is the same four objects:

| Object | What it is | Who feeds it |
|---|---|---|
| **Contacts** | people and companies | imported, typed in, or synced from forms and lead sources |
| **Pipeline** | stages a deal moves through (New → Contacted → Qualified → Proposal → Won/Lost) | a human drags the card |
| **Activities** | calls, emails, notes, tasks, logged against a contact | a human logs them (or an email integration logs some) |
| **Automation** | "when X happens, do Y": sequences, action plans, reminders, assignments | a human designs the rule, then a human does what the reminder says |

Around that: integrations (lead sources in, calendar and email out), reporting (how many at each stage), and
the business model, which is **per seat**: the CRM makes money when there are more humans using it.

**Why that model breaks for a one-person business:** the human is the engine, and the one-person business
has no spare human.

- 79 percent of opportunity data gathered by reps is never entered into the CRM (ESNA, via Nutshell; C).
- 71 percent of reps say they spend too much time on data entry; 68 percent call it their most
  time-consuming task; about 2 percent trust the data (review-site compilations; C).
- Fewer than 37 percent of reps use their CRM daily (C). Manual data entry is the top-cited reason users
  abandon a deployment (C).
- 30 to 63 percent of CRM implementations fail to meet their goals; more than 60 percent of failures are
  people problems, not software (C).
- 80 percent of sales need five or more follow-ups; most businesses stop after one or two (widely repeated,
  original source unverified; C, treat as directional).

The CRM gives the owner a place to *record* following up. It does not *do* the following up. That gap is the
whole reason FollowUp exists, and it is also the reason "a simpler CRM" is the wrong product: a simpler place
to not enter data is still a place to not enter data.

## 3. Why we are making this

The mission, in the founder's words, is in `PRODUCT_DIRECTION.md`: *a business owner should not lose a lead
because of not following up, not following up in time, or not following up correctly.* The evidence that this
is a real, measured problem:

- **Median response time to a web lead is 42 hours** across 2,241 US firms and 100,000-plus leads
  (Oldroyd, McElheran, Elkington, Harvard Business Review; **A** for the study's own number).
- **Contacting within an hour** makes a firm nearly 7 times as likely to qualify the lead as waiting an
  hour more, and 60-plus times as likely as waiting a day (HBR, 1.25 million leads at 42 firms; **A**).
- **Within five minutes** the odds of reaching the lead are 100 times higher than at 30 minutes, and of
  qualifying it 21 times higher (Oldroyd / InsideSales 2007, 15,000 leads; **A** for the study, old).
- **27 percent of calls to home-services businesses go unanswered** (vendor blog; **C**).
- Earlier passes on file: owners already pay $450 to $5,800 a month for the person or the suite that does
  this job (`2026-09-07-why-followup-evidence-for-and-against.md` §3).

So: the lead is lost in the first hours, and the owner with no staff is the one who cannot answer in the
first hours. Every CRM above assumes someone will. FollowUp's bet is that the *doing* can be automated, with
the owner keeping the decision.

## 4. Is this even valuable? The honest answer

**Yes, the problem is worth money.** Podium charges $400 to $1,000 a month and reports $100 million in AI
agent revenue inside two years (C). Structurely was bought by a public holding company in January for its
nine years of follow-up conversations. Beside raised $32 million to answer missed calls. People pay.

**But the thing most people think FollowUp is, "AI that replies to your leads", is being commoditised right
now,** and the research already said this on 2026-09-07:

- Meta Business Agent: free inbound AI on WhatsApp, Instagram and Messenger since June 2026 (6a on file).
- Jobber: an AI receptionist for $29 a month, inside the tool its customers already pay for.
- HubSpot: AI drafting on the free tier. Follow Up Boss: native AI drafting since 2025. Gmail: Gemini
  drafts and three-day nudges.

Each of those answers the *first* message. **None of them, as far as any pass has found, does what happens
after:** notices the customer went quiet after being answered, writes the second and fifth message in the
owner's voice across email, text and DM, stops the second the customer replies, and keeps the record. Verse
has done a version of it for twelve years at team prices and never came down-market.

**Where the value actually is, then:**

1. The owner who has **no CRM and will never fill one in**. FollowUp reads the inbox they already have.
   There is nothing to enter. (Rule 1 and the data-entry evidence in §1.)
2. The **follow-through**, not the first reply. Day 3, 7, 14, 30. This is the part nobody gives away.
3. **Control.** 79 percent of people prefer a human in customer service (6c on file). FollowUp's answer is
   "every reply waits for your OK" by default, in the owner's own words. That is a feature the AI-receptionist
   companies cannot easily copy without changing what they sell.
4. **Every language.** The multilingual owner in Toronto is underserved by every tool above (all of them are
   English-first; verified only by absence, so C).
5. **Price and shape.** $0 to $39 flat, no seats, no contract, no setup call. Every competitor above is 3 to
   30 times that or sales-gated.

**What could make it not valuable** (unchanged from 09-07, plus one): Meta or Google adds *proactive*
follow-up for free; consumers reject machine-written messages even with a human behind them; one developer;
and the new one from this week's production data: **owners who never used a CRM may not open *any* tool
daily**, and FollowUp's Today only works if they do. Four outside testers, one active, is the only real
number we have on that, and it is not yet a good one.

## 5. Can it scale?

**Costs scale well.** From `2026-09-09-business-model-case.md` §8 (estimates, checked against the code):

| | 10 customers | 100 | 500 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue at $29 | $290 | $2,900 | $14,500 |
| Gross margin | ~76–79% | ~79–89% | ~69–89% |

Twilio and Meta usage is the customer's own cost, not ours; AI text runs about $0.15 to $0.30 per customer a
month. Breakeven: about 6 customers with no founder salary, about 130 with a $3,000 one, about 330 with one
hire. At $39 (the Plus price now in code) every number improves.

**Acquisition is the constraint, not cost.** LTV:CAC is 8:1 or better while the founder sells by hand, and
drops to 2:1 or 3:1 under paid acquisition at $29. So the path is: founder-led to the first 100, price at
$39-plus, prove churn is low because the record is sticky (Rule 2), *then* paid channels.

**The market is big enough that none of this is the limit.** Ontario alone has about 410,000 small employer
businesses and Canada about 2 million self-employed (ISED / Statistics Canada; **A**). The US has 36 million
small businesses, 82 percent with no employees (SBA; **A**). The business-model case bounds the reachable
market at roughly 1.2 million US businesses in the two clearest segments. We need 100, then 1,000.

**What scales badly:** support (the one swing factor in the margin table), platform verification per
channel (Google OAuth review, Meta app review, each a weeks-long gate we are in right now), and compliance
per message type (CASL, PIPEDA, TCPA, A2P 10DLC).

## 6. Who the competitors are, grouped

- **Generalist CRMs** (HubSpot, Pipedrive, Zoho): cheap per seat, free tiers, AI drafting. They will keep
  adding AI to the *inside* of the CRM. They will not come for the owner who refuses to use a CRM.
- **All-in-one agency platforms** (GoHighLevel): everything, for people who configure things for a living.
  Six weeks to learn. The anti-FollowUp.
- **Vertical operations suites** (Leap, Jobber, Housecall Pro, Follow Up Boss, Lofty): own the daily workflow
  of one trade and bolt answering on. Strong where the owner already lives in them; nothing for the owner
  who does not.
- **Messaging and reputation suites** (Podium): good product, priced and contracted for bigger local
  businesses; reactive.
- **AI follow-up specialists** (Structurely, Ylopo, Verse): the closest in *job*; all real estate or
  mortgage, all team-priced, all sales-gated.
- **Horizontal AI receptionists** (Beside, Goodcall, Bravi): the closest in *buyer*; all phone-first,
  reactive, no follow-through found. Beside is the one to watch: funded, horizontal, one decision away.
- **Platforms giving it away** (Meta Business Agent, Gmail Gemini, Jobber's $29 receptionist): the
  first-reply layer is theirs. Don't fight there.

## 7. Why someone buys FollowUp instead

**True today (shipped, in the code):**
- Nothing to fill in. Connect Gmail, it reads the leads already there, and new ones land on Today.
- Every reply is written in the owner's voice and **waits for their OK** by default (A-070). The owner can
  turn on "handle it all" per customer, any time.
- It **stops the moment the customer answers** (tested guarantee), and says why it held each message.
- Follow-up on day 3, 7, 14, 30 if they go quiet, then it stops.
- Any language in, the same language out.
- A record of what it did, per customer, that the owner can read.
- Booking link and calendar; website form; Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp as sources.
- $0 during the beta, $39 / $79 after; no seats, no contract.

**Not true yet (the gap between the pitch and the product):**
- The proof number: "N customers answered because FollowUp followed up" on a page the owner can show.
  This was named the moat on 2026-09-07 and is still not on a screen.
- The cross-channel "went quiet after being answered" score, as a push, not a filter.
- Voice (building, parked).
- Daily use by strangers. One active tester.

**Not a reason, and we should stop saying it:** "AI writes your replies". Everyone has that now.

## 8. How to make this product work

1. **Ten owners who open Today every day.** Not features. The Monday 2026-10-06 meeting with the one active
   tester is the first real observation; the question to ask is "what did you do when you saw the reply
   waiting?", not "do you like it?".
2. **Pick the shortest path to those ten, without going vertical in the product.** The product stays
   horizontal (Rule 1, decided). The *selling* can still start where Gmail is the inbox and the owner
   answers alone: solo realtors and home-services owners in the GTA, many of them multilingual. That is who
   the existing research says is underserved; it is also who the founder can reach in person.
3. **One number per customer, on Today's foot and in a weekly email:** replies sent within the hour, and
   customers who answered after a FollowUp check-in. It is the proof, the pricing argument and the thing to
   show the next owner.
4. **Price at $39, not $29** (on file, 2026-09-11), and say the alternative costs $400.
5. **Don't compete for the first reply on Meta channels or against Jobber.** Treat them as sources. Own the
   follow-through, the owner's OK, the language, and the record.
6. **Keep the product this simple.** The three screens shipped this week are the right shape; the data in
   §1 says complexity is why the incumbents lose the solo owner. Everything added from here should pass "does
   this change what the owner does next?".

## What I'm unsure about

- Every competitor price is a 2026 snippet from a review site unless marked B; vendors change plans often.
- The CRM-adoption and data-entry statistics are repeated across vendor blogs; the original studies could
  not be read here. They agree with each other and with the founder's own experience, which is why they are
  used, at grade C.
- The biggest unknown is not in any table: whether the owner who has never used a tool will use this one
  daily. Only testers answer that.
