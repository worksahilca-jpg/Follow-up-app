# What to send when staying in touch

**Question (founder, 2026-10-15):** for customers who said yes to "Stay in touch" (A-260), what kind of note
actually gets a reply? Is an offer always the best thing to lead with, or is something else more useful?

**Short answer:** an offer is strong only when it really fits. What the evidence favours is a note that is *about
them*: set off by something they did or said, and that says why it matters to them. Name-only personalisation and
broadcast messages do worst.

Builds on, without repeating: `2026-09-09-followup-cadence-best-practices.md` (cadence, "just checking in" as the
thing not to send), `2026-09-15-reaching-back-out-to-ignored-leads.md` (re-contact after a failure, unverified
"breakup email" stats), `reports/Realtor replies that win trust.md` (first replies).

Grades: **A** peer-reviewed, **B** large dataset with a stated method, **C** vendor or coach claim, **D** anecdote.

## 1. What the evidence says

1. **Relevance beats personal detail.** White, Zahay, Thorbjørnsen & Shavitt, "Getting too personal", *Marketing
   Letters* 19(1), 2008: personalised emails worked when the offer had real value *and* said quickly how the
   person's own details related to it. "If the offer was valuable and justified, the level of personal information
   didn't matter." Heavy personal detail without that reason backfired. **A** (student sample).
2. **Greetings by name don't help; tips based on what they did do.** Wattal (Temple University), about 10 million
   marketing emails to 600,000 customers: personalised greetings drew mostly negative reactions; recommendations
   based on past purchases drew positive ones, most for people who knew the firm least. **B** (reported via a
   press release).
3. **Expected relevance raises response and lowers fatigue.** Micheaux, *Journal of Advertising*, 2011, about
   15,000 consumers at a credit company: when people expected messages to be relevant, response rose and
   fatigue fell, even at a higher sending rate. **A**, cited second-hand.
4. **Messages set off by the customer beat scheduled ones.** Epsilon / Email Institute, Q3 2014: triggered emails
   had open rates 76.7% higher and click rates 151.9% higher than other emails. NP Digital, April 2025, 162M+
   emails from 30 companies: triggered 56.1% open and 4.4% click, against 28.5% and 2.3% for scheduled. **B/C**
   (vendor-run, but consistent with each other).

## 2. What practitioners do (weaker evidence)

- **Home value for owners.** Homebot sends homeowners a monthly report on their home's value and equity, branded
  to the agent; it advertises a 75% average open rate. **C** (vendor figure, opens are inflated by Apple Mail).
- **Useful things, not only listings.** Buffini's monthly "Item of Value" covers home care, money, local market
  updates and more, so the agent is useful between deals. **C** (coaching method, no published data).
- **One plain question.** Dean Jackson's "9-word email" ("Are you still looking for a house in [area]?") got
  "dozens of replies" for one agent; one team's re-engagement texts reached 65% replies on a new-search prompt
  (Inman, 2017). **D**. The founder rejected this style as boring (R-118), so it is listed, not proposed.
- **Why it matters long term.** NAR 2025 Profile, via a secondary source: 43% of buyers and 66% of sellers chose a
  referred agent or one they had worked with before. **C** (check the NAR original before quoting).

## 3. What this means for FollowUp

Best to worst, for a note to someone staying in touch:

1. **Their own moment.** Something they told you comes due: "You said you'd talk to the bank in January. Rates
   moved this week. Want the three questions buyers I work with ask their lender?" (Findings 1, 3, 4.)
2. **A real match, with the reason.** A listing, price drop or opening that fits what they asked for, saying why:
   under their budget, near the school they named. (Finding 1.) This is the founder's "another offer".
3. **Something useful for their situation.** What similar homes near them sold for, a rate change, a new program,
   a seasonal tip. Only from the owner's words. (Buffini; finding 3.)
4. **Never:** a note with nothing in it, a greeting-only "personal" touch, a blast to everyone, fake urgency, or a
   reason FollowUp made up.

Every kind ends with one easy question and how to stop (CASL needs an unsubscribe in every commercial message).

**What FollowUp does today:** it writes only when the owner shares news, so kinds 2 and 3 work now. Kind 1 needs
something new: remembering when a customer said they'd be ready ("January", "when our lease ends in March") and
writing then. That changes behaviour, so it needs the founder's yes.
