# Pre-PMF on-track / off-track signals, and the evidence on building before users

_Researched 2026-11-01 for FollowUp (followupbase.io). Evidence-strength tags: **[Strong]** = primary source or widely corroborated data; **[Moderate]** = a named expert's view reported through a reliable summary, or a single primary opinion; **[Weak]** = vendor blog, anecdote, or one unverified secondary source._

_Access limitation: in this session the hosts paulgraham.com, pmarchive.com, cbinsights.com and lennysnewsletter.com could not be fetched (session network allowlist). Every quote below comes from search-engine extracts of those pages or from secondary summaries. Quotes marked "(verify wording)" should be checked against the original before being published or quoted to anyone else._

_FollowUp's numbers as briefed (as of Nov 1 2026): 9 outside signups since September (1 in the last 30 days); 5 customers captured in 30 days across 3 businesses; 1 customer answered through the product in 30 days; 0 bookings; about 1 paying. Effort over the last 6 weeks: mostly polish, redesigns, AI accuracy tests and internal admin tooling._

## 1. What do YC, First Round, Lenny, Superhuman/Sean Ellis, Andreessen and Rob Walling say are signs of being off track before PMF?

### Takeaway
The canonical sources agree on one off-track picture: usage that has to be pushed rather than pulled, low or flat growth in revenue or active users, no word of mouth, and founders who work on the product instead of getting users by hand. FollowUp matches nearly every item on that list. Several sources also say it takes years to reach PMF, so the worry is less where FollowUp is after about two months and more what the founder is spending time on.

### Cited Findings
- **Paul Graham, "Startup = Growth" (2012):** a good growth rate during YC is 5-7% a week, 10% a week is exceptional, and "if you can only manage 1%, it's a sign you haven't yet figured out what you're doing" (verify wording). The metric to watch is revenue, or active users for products that don't charge yet. **[Strong: many independent copies of the same lines]** — [Kamil's summary of the essay](https://kamilski81.blogspot.com/2014/05/startup-growth-by-paul-graham-summary.html); [Equidam benchmarks citing PG](https://www.equidam.com/benchmarks-for-startup-revenue-growth); original at paulgraham.com/growth.html (not fetchable this session)
- **Paul Graham, "Do Things That Don't Scale" (2013):** "The most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually. Nearly all startups have to." / "You can't wait for users to come to you. You have to go out and get them." Stripe's founders installed the product on users' laptops themselves, which YC calls the "Collison installation." **[Strong]** — [Business Insider on the essay](https://www.businessinsider.in/paul-graham-offers-counter-intuitive-advice-to-startups-do-things-that-dont-scale/articleshow/21086175.cms); [Indie Hackers summary](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/summary-do-things-that-don-t-scale-by-paul-graham-bdcb54215a); [paulgraham.com/ds.html](https://paulgraham.com/ds.HTML)
- **Same essay, a second passage (from memory, not re-verified this session):** founders resist recruiting users partly from "shyness and laziness"; they'd "rather sit at home writing code than go out and talk to a bunch of strangers." This passage fits FollowUp's situation closely, so check the exact wording before quoting it. **[Strong source, wording unverified]** — [paulgraham.com/ds.html](https://paulgraham.com/ds.HTML)
- **Marc Andreessen, "The only thing that matters" (2007):** "You can always feel when product/market fit isn't happening." The symptoms he lists: customers aren't really getting value, word of mouth isn't spreading, usage isn't growing fast, press reviews are "blah," the sales cycle is long and deals don't close. With PMF, customers buy as fast as you can make the product. (verify the exact list wording at pmarchive.com/guide_to_startups_part4.html) **[Strong: original widely reproduced, small wording differences between copies]** — [Bokardo on the origin of PMF](https://bokardo.com/archives/origin-product-market-fit/); [Sachin Rekhi](https://www.sachinrekhi.com/p/how-do-you-know-if-youve-achieved-product-market-fit); [Wikipedia: Product-market fit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product-market_fit)
- **Andreessen's prescription (from memory, wording unverified):** do "whatever is required" to get to PMF, including rewriting the product or moving to a different market. **[Moderate]** — [SK Murphy archive of Andreessen's essays](https://www.skmurphy.com/blog/2009/11/02/marc-andreessens-entrepreneurial-insights-rescued-from-bit-rot-by-jed-christiansen/)
- **Lenny Rachitsky, "push vs pull":** before PMF, founders "had to push their ideas on people and nag them to use the product." Pull looks like organic growth picking up, customers asking to pay before you ask, and users frustrated by what's missing rather than excited by what exists. **[Moderate: Lenny's synthesis of founder interviews]** — [Lenny via Product Hunt, "What it feels like when you've found PMF"](https://www.producthunt.com/stories/what-it-feels-like-when-you-ve-found-product-market-fit-by-lenny-rachitsky)
- **Lenny on pivoting:** quotes the idea that if you have to ask whether you have PMF, or whether to pivot, you probably already know the answer. **[Moderate; paywalled, seen only as an extract]** — [Lenny's Newsletter, "The art of the pivot, part 2"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-art-of-the-pivot-part-2-how-why)
- **Todd Jackson / First Round, the four levels of PMF:** Nascent, Developing, Strong, Extreme, scored on satisfaction, demand and efficiency. Level 1 (Nascent) is roughly 3-5 customers who are highly satisfied. Summaries disagree on the higher levels: one has L2 at 5→25 customers and L3 at 100+; another has L3 at 25-100 and L4 at 100+. The levers for moving between levels are the "Four Ps": Persona, Problem, Promise, Product. One summary says about 60% of startups "never gonna get past L2." **[Moderate: mostly AI-generated podcast summaries; check thresholds against First Round's own page]** — [Lenny's Podcast episode page](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/a-framework-for-finding-product-market); [One Cerebral summary](https://onecerebral.beehiiv.com/p/this-vc-s-framework-to-finding-product-market-fit); [First Round](https://www.firstround.com/levels); [Antoine Buteau notes](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-todd-jackson/)
- **Expected time at each level (Jackson, via summaries):** L1 about 12-18 months, L2 about a year, the whole path 4-6 years. **[Weak-Moderate: summary only]** — [Recall.it summary](https://www.recall.it/summary/startup/a-framework-for-finding-product-market-fit-or-todd-jackson-first-round-capital)
- **Sean Ellis 40% test:** Ellis benchmarked hundreds of startups. The ones that struggle to grow "always get less than 40% 'very disappointed'," and the ones that grow most easily "almost always get more than 40%." **[Strong: primary write-up by Vohra]** — [Superhuman, "How Superhuman built an engine to find PMF"](https://blog.superhuman.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)
- **Rahul Vohra / Superhuman:** started at 22% "very disappointed" ("it was clear it had not reached product-market fit"), compared against Slack's 51%. The method: segment to the users who would be very disappointed, double down on what they love, fix the objections of the somewhat-disappointed group, and "politely disregard" users who wouldn't be disappointed. **[Strong]** — [Superhuman blog](https://blog.superhuman.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/); [Business of Software talk](https://businessofsoftware.org/talks/product-market-fit-engine/)
- **Rob Walling / MicroConf:** for an unlaunched founder, the biggest risk "isn't failing, it's spending months on something nobody wants." In an AMA, Walling said he would not build a product and then look for 10 customers; he would find the customers first. **[Moderate: MicroConf page plus AMA summary]** — [MicroConf by-stage page](https://microconf.com/by-stage); [Rob Walling Indie Hackers AMA](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/i-started-drip-microconf-and-tinyseed-ask-me-anything-64642c843b)
- **Michael Seibel (YC):** get a "functional, albeit flawed" product into users' hands within the first two months. Talk to users before deciding what to build, because "the real problem is often not what you think it is." Avoid "fake work," and "don't be fake Steve Jobs; iterate and talk to customers." **[Moderate: YC library page plus talk summaries]** — [YC Library, "How to build an MVP"](https://ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp); [Antoine Buteau, lessons from Michael Seibel](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-michael-seibel/)
- **Dalton Caldwell (YC):** do customer validation before polishing; growth hacking and heavy A/B testing are "a total waste of time" for very early startups. **[Moderate: podcast summary]** — [Lenny's Podcast with Dalton Caldwell](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/lessons-from-1000-yc-startups); [One Cerebral summary](https://onecerebral.beehiiv.com/p/lessons-from-1-000-yc-startups)

### Inferences
- **FollowUp against the checklist:**
  - Andreessen's symptoms: usage not growing and no word of mouth both apply. Signups fell from about 8 in September to early October to 1 in the last 30 days.
  - Lenny's push vs pull: this is push.
  - PG's growth test: weekly growth in active businesses is about zero or negative, which is below his 1% "haven't figured out what you're doing" line.
  - Jackson's levels: FollowUp is below Level 1 (Nascent). It does not yet have 3-5 businesses getting regular value; 3 businesses captured 5 customers between them in 30 days, with 1 reply and 0 bookings.
- **The Sean Ellis test can't be run yet.** There are too few active users for the 40% figure to mean anything (see section 3). Asking the 3 active businesses the "very disappointed" question one-on-one is still useful as a qualitative signal.
- **Separate the stage from the behaviour.** The stage is normal: the median B2B company takes about 2 years to feel PMF (section 5), and Jackson's L1 takes 12-18 months. The behaviour is the off-track signal: 6 weeks spent mostly on polish and admin tooling while signups fell. Every source above says to spend this stage recruiting users by hand.

### Gaps
- Jackson's exact customer-count thresholds for L2-L4 differ between summaries. First Round's own article couldn't be read.
- Exact wording of Andreessen's symptom list and PG's "shyness and laziness" passage was not checked against the originals (domains blocked).
- No primary Seibel post confirming the often-repeated "a founder has two jobs: build and talk to users" line. Searches didn't find it, so don't attribute it to him.

## 2. What is the evidence that building or polishing before having users is a common failure mode?

### Takeaway
The best datasets, CB Insights' post-mortem studies and the Startup Genome Report, both put building something without validated demand at or near the top of startup failure causes. Startup Genome specifically found that over-building in the discovery phase is typical of startups that fail. Both datasets are old (2011-2021), self-reported or survey-based, and have known biases, so read them as strong directional evidence rather than precise numbers.

### Cited Findings
- **CB Insights (101 post-mortems, ~2017-2018):** "no market need" was the most cited reason, at 42%. Next came running out of cash (29%), not the right team (23%) and being outcompeted (19%). Founders usually gave more than one reason, so the percentages add up to more than 100%. **[Strong as a widely cited dataset; methodological caveats below]** — [Startup News AU on CB Insights](https://startupnews.com.au/news/top-20-reasons-startups-fail-cb-insights/); [Inc., "101 failed startups tell all"](https://www.inc.com/kimberly-weisul/what-went-wrong-failed-startups-tell-all.html); [CB Insights PDF mirror](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/1707/0685/files/CB-Insights_Top-Reasons-Startups-Fail.pdf)
- **CB Insights 2021 update (111 post-mortems since 2018):** 35% cited no market need. The often-repeated "38% ran out of cash / couldn't raise" figure was not confirmed in this session. CB Insights notes there is "rarely one reason" a startup fails. **[Moderate]** — [SmartCompany](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/startupsmart/analysis/startups-fail-founders-capital/); [LaunchRock summary](https://www.launchrock.com/library/expert-advice/why-startups-fail)
- **Startup Genome Report (2011-2012, about 3,200 startups):** "about 70% scaled prematurely." Premature scaling was the leading cause of failure: 74% of high-growth internet startups that failed did so because of it, and no startup that scaled prematurely passed 100,000 users. Startups that scaled at the right time grew about 20x faster. **[Moderate-Strong: large sample but self-reported survey; numbers only seen via secondary coverage]** — [Startup Genome, "Why startups fail: premature scaling"](https://startupgenome.com/report/why-startups-fail-premature-scaling/why-startups-fail-premature-scaling); [GeekWire 2011](https://www.geekwire.com/2011/number-reason-startups-fail-premature-scaling/); [Startup Genome deep dive](https://startupgenome.com/insights/a-deep-dive-into-the-anatomy-of-premature-scaling)
- **Startup Genome on over-building:** startups that scaled prematurely ("inconsistent") wrote 3.4x more lines of code in the discovery phase and 2.25x more in the efficiency stage. **[Weak-Moderate: only in a secondary compilation]** — [Startupgeist summary (Medium)](https://medium.com/startupgeist-articles/startup-failure-why-startups-fail-e6c4856b65a7); [Young Upstarts 2011](https://youngupstarts.com/2011/08/30/inconsistency-and-premature-scaling-main-cause-for-startup-failure-blackbox)
- **Startup Genome on pivoting:** startups that pivoted once or twice raised 2.5x more money, had 3.6x better user growth and were 52% less likely to scale prematurely than those that pivoted more than twice or never. **[Weak-Moderate: secondary]** — [Startupgeist, "Why startups succeed"](https://medium.com/startupgeist-articles/startup-success-why-startups-succeed-8bc397493a7b); [Flying Penguin](https://www.flyingpenguin.com/risk-lessons-from-the-startup-genome-project/)
- **Startup Genome's definition of premature scaling (from memory, not re-verified this session):** it includes the product side: building before problem/solution fit, investing in scalability or perfecting the product past "good enough" before PMF, and adding nice-to-have features. Verify against the report before quoting. **[Unverified detail]** — [Startup Genome report](https://startupgenome.com/report/why-startups-fail-premature-scaling/why-startups-fail-premature-scaling)
- **Caveats on the data:** Startup Genome's data was collected in 2010-2012. The report later published a clarification about what it means by premature scaling, and one critic argues the analysis mixes up "bad" with "failed." **[Moderate]** — [Startup Genome, "Premature scaling: a deep dive"](https://startupgenome.com/insights/premature-scaling-a-deep-dive); [Alexander Jarvis critique](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/do-some-startups-fail-because-they-try-to-scale-too-quickly/)
- **MicroConf (bootstrapped and solo SaaS founders):** the biggest risk for an unlaunched founder is "spending months on something nobody wants." **[Moderate]** — [MicroConf by-stage](https://microconf.com/by-stage)

### Inferences
- FollowUp's last six weeks are a textbook case of what Startup Genome calls product-side premature scaling. AI accuracy test suites, admin tooling and redesigns are investments in quality and scalability, made before 3-5 businesses rely on the product. This is the "write more code in discovery" pattern.
- The CB Insights "no market need" category is a warning, not a verdict. FollowUp has not shown that there is no need; it has run too few real interactions (1 reply, 0 bookings) to tell. The risk is spending months more polishing without learning which of the two it is.

### Gaps
- No rigorous (non-self-reported) study that isolates "polish before users" as a cause of failure. The evidence is survey and post-mortem based, with survivorship and self-attribution bias.
- The original Startup Genome PDF and CB Insights' page could not be fetched, so the 3.4x / 3.6x numbers rest on secondary summaries.
- No good 2023-2026 replication of either dataset was found.

## 3. What are concrete early benchmarks (weekly growth, active users after N weeks, usage that signals real pull, customer conversations per week)?

### Takeaway
The firm numbers are PG's 5-7% weekly growth, the Sean Ellis 40% "very disappointed" bar (which needs about 40+ engaged respondents), Lenny's SMB SaaS retention benchmarks, and First Round's L1 = 3-5 highly satisfied customers. For how many conversations a founder should have each week, there is no official YC figure; the available numbers (daily, or about 11-20 a week) come from vendor surveys and are weak.

### Cited Findings
- **Weekly growth:** 5-7% a week is good during YC, 10% is exceptional, and 1% means "you haven't yet figured out what you're doing." Measure revenue, or active users if not charging. Compounding: 1% a week is about 1.7x a year, 5% a week about 12.6x. **[Strong]** — [Kamil's summary of PG](https://kamilski81.blogspot.com/2014/05/startup-growth-by-paul-graham-summary.html); [KrASIA](https://kr-asia.com/checkitout-if-you-get-growth-everything-else-tends-to-fall-into-place)
- **Counterpoint:** TechCrunch (2016) argued that growth can be "a false signal in Y Combinator startups." Only the headline was seen; content not reviewed. **[Unreviewed]** — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2016/12/18/growth-as-a-false-signal-in-y-combinator-startups/?ncid=rss)
- **Sean Ellis 40% bar:** described in section 1. Practitioners say the result is only meaningful with about 40 or more responses from users who have used the product at least twice in the last two weeks; below that, treat it as directional. These rules are practitioner conventions, not Ellis's own specification. **[40% bar Strong; sample rules Weak]** — [Superhuman](https://blog.superhuman.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/); [Koji guide](https://www.koji.so/docs/sean-ellis-test-product-market-fit); [Learning Loop](https://learningloop.io/plays/product-market-fit-survey)
- **Retention, SMB and mid-market SaaS (Lenny):** about 60% user retention is good and about 80% is great (consumer SaaS about 40% / 70%). Net revenue retention: about 90% good, 110% great. Monthly churn: 2.5-5% is good for SMB. Based on a survey of about 20 growth practitioners, around 2020-2022. **[Moderate]** — [Lenny, "What is good retention"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-good-retention-issue-29); [Lenny reading notes on monthly churn](https://personal-learnings.pages.dev/reading_room/issues/lennys_newsletter/0510_what-is-good-monthly-churn.html)
- **Customer counts (First Round, Jackson):** L1 Nascent means about 3-5 customers who are highly satisfied. **[Moderate]** — [One Cerebral](https://onecerebral.beehiiv.com/p/this-vc-s-framework-to-finding-product-market-fit)
- **Signs of pull (Lenny):** an inflection in organic growth, customers asking to pay before you ask, and users frustrated about what's missing. **[Moderate]** — [Product Hunt / Lenny](https://www.producthunt.com/stories/what-it-feels-like-when-you-ve-found-product-market-fit-by-lenny-rachitsky)
- **Customer conversations per week:**
  - Kraftful (a YC company) surveyed YC founders: 60% talk to users daily; product builders outside YC mostly talk to users weekly (88%).
  - Perspective AI (vendor, 2026): founders averaged about 11 conversations a week in the 6 months before their Series A and 1.4 a week after.
  - Koji (vendor) attributes "15-20 a week" to Eric Migicovsky's YC talk; this attribution is unverified.

  **[Weak: all vendor sources]** — [Kraftful](https://www.kraftful.com/blogs/how-yc-founders-talk-to-users); [Perspective AI](https://getperspective.ai/blog/talk-to-your-customers-most-ignored-advice-b2b-saas-2026); [Koji](https://www.koji.so/docs/how-to-talk-to-users-yc-method)
- **Funnel for the first 10 customers:** one estimate says landing 10 paying B2B customers usually means real conversations with 50-150 people. **[Weak: single third-party estimate]** — [echai.ventures](https://echai.ventures/startingup/first-customers/how-many-people-should-i-actually-reach-out-to-before-i-expect-to-close-my-first-10-paying-customers)

### Inferences
- **For FollowUp, the right unit is weekly active businesses with real leads flowing through the product.** The simplest version: number of businesses with at least 1 lead captured and at least 1 reply sent through FollowUp in the week. Today that is about 1 business with a reply in a month. Signups are a vanity measure at this stage.
- **A defensible near-term bar** (my synthesis, combining Jackson L1 and PG; not a published threshold): within about 6-8 weeks, reach 3-5 realtors who each have leads flowing weekly and say they would be very disappointed to lose FollowUp, with at least 3 paying. After that, aim for the weekly-active-business count to grow 5%+ a week.
- **Conversation volume:** the vendor data clusters around daily, or about 10-20 a week, before PMF. For a solo founder whose product needs real conversations to happen (here, realtors' leads), 10-15 customer conversations a week is a reasonable floor to adopt. This is an inference, not a sourced number.

### Gaps
- No credible published benchmark for "N active users after N weeks" for B2B SMB products at pre-seed. PG's growth rate is the only widely accepted proxy.
- Lenny's retention benchmarks are paywalled, so the time horizon (for example month 6) wasn't confirmed this session.
- No primary YC source with a numeric target for customer conversations per week.

## 4. When should a founder stop building and switch to selling or talking to users? What is a good weekly time split at this stage?

### Takeaway
The sources agree: as soon as a flawed but working version exists (Seibel: within about 2 months), the founder's main job becomes recruiting users by hand and doing things that don't scale, and building should follow what those users say. No reputable source gives a precise percentage time split; the 50/50 heuristic that does circulate is weakly sourced.

### Cited Findings
- **Seibel / YC:** get a functional, flawed version into users' hands within about 2 months, and talk to users before deciding what to build. **[Moderate]** — [YC Library, "How to build an MVP"](https://ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp)
- **PG:** recruiting users manually is what "nearly all startups have to" do at the start; "you can't wait for users to come to you." Stripe's "Collison installation" meant personally setting the product up for users. Lukewarm early users can be served by the founders operating the software for them. **[Strong]** — [Business Insider on PG](https://www.businessinsider.in/paul-graham-offers-counter-intuitive-advice-to-startups-do-things-that-dont-scale/articleshow/21086175.cms); [Indie Hackers summary](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/summary-do-things-that-don-t-scale-by-paul-graham-bdcb54215a)
- **Rob Walling:** find the first customers before building, not after. **[Moderate]** — [Indie Hackers AMA](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/i-started-drip-microconf-and-tinyseed-ask-me-anything-64642c843b)
- **Lenny (first 10 B2B customers):** start with your personal network, looking for people who match your ideal customer, then do careful cold outbound and use investors' networks. Examples: Gusto's first customers were "friends we knew who were just starting their businesses"; Figma's founder cold-emailed or got introductions to people he admired. **[Moderate: paywalled, extract only]** — [Lenny, "How to find and win your first 10 B2B customers"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-win-your-first-10-b2b-customers); [Lenny, "How today's fastest-growing B2B businesses found their first ten customers"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-todays-fastest-growing-b2b-businesses)
- **Caldwell:** validate with customers before polishing anything; early A/B testing and growth hacking are a waste of time. **[Moderate]** — [One Cerebral on Dalton Caldwell](https://onecerebral.beehiiv.com/p/lessons-from-1-000-yc-startups)
- **Time split:** the only number found was an "equal time on product and go-to-market from day one" (50/50) heuristic, from a low-quality lessons aggregator. Other founder pieces say selling is the only market feedback that is "truly actionable." **[Weak]** — [Sliplane founder-mindset lessons](https://onepage-research.sliplane.app/lessons/founder-mindset/fm-p536); [Rho, "Focus on selling, not building"](https://www.rho.co/blog/founder-takes-focus-on-selling-not-building)

### Inferences
- **FollowUp has passed every "stop building" trigger in these sources.** It has a working product, outside signups, one paying customer and live channels. What is missing is enough real usage to learn from. So the next unit of progress is a conversation, an onboarding or a done-for-you setup, not a commit.
- **Suggested split for the next 4-6 weeks** (my synthesis, not a sourced number): about 70-80% of founder hours on customer-facing work (outreach, setup calls, doing it for them, sitting with realtors while they use it, follow-ups). About 20-30% on building, limited to fixes or features that a named, active user is blocked on. No new polish, redesign or admin tooling unless a live customer needs it. This is more sales-heavy than the weakly sourced 50/50 because FollowUp has had about 6 weeks of 80%+ building with flat usage.
- **A useful rule:** every build task should name the specific realtor it unblocks. If it can't, it waits.

### Gaps
- No primary YC, First Round or MicroConf source gives a percentage time split for a pre-PMF solo founder.
- The content of Rob Walling's March 2026 "2/20/200 Validation Framework" episode wasn't found. It may contain specific numbers; worth checking at [MicroConf by-stage](https://microconf.com/by-stage).

## 5. How should a founder decide between persisting, narrowing (niche or wedge), or pivoting? Are there decision rules or timelines?

### Takeaway
PMF usually takes years (median about 2 years in B2B), so less than 3 months without traction does not justify a pivot. The sources say to narrow and change one variable at a time (persona, problem, promise, product), pivot toward what you know, and avoid both never pivoting and pivoting repeatedly. Widely quoted rules like "talk to 100 customers" or "10 paying in 90 days" could not be traced to a primary source.

### Cited Findings
- **Lenny:** the median time from idea to feeling PMF was about 2 years. In B2B, "it normally takes two years to start feeling product-market fit." Figma, Airtable and Slack took 4+ years but are exceptions. Of 25 companies studied, about half found PMF soon after launch and half spent months or years iterating. **[Moderate-Strong: based on 20+ founder interviews]** — [Lenny, "Finding product-market fit"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/finding-product-market-fit); [Lenny, "Time to PMF"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/time-to-product-market-fit); [Lenny thread](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1310972520812539905)
- **Jackson / First Round:** move between PMF levels by changing one of the Four Ps (Persona, Problem, Promise, Product). Jackson's levels also expect L1 to take about 12-18 months. **[Moderate]** — [Lenny's Podcast with Todd Jackson](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/a-framework-for-finding-product-market); [Notion notes on the framework](https://taylormcgann.notion.site/Framework-for-Product-Market-Fit-556c562123674253b2d1167ef4a428e2)
- **Caldwell / YC:**
  - "Just don't die": stay alive long enough to keep taking good attempts.
  - "A good pivot is like going home": pivot toward the founders' own expertise.
  - Avoid "tar pit" ideas.
  - In a YC discussion with Eric Migicovsky, he talks about telling a product problem apart from weak sales effort before deciding to pivot.

  **[Moderate]** — [Lenny's Podcast with Dalton Caldwell](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/lessons-from-1000-yc-startups); [Summify, YC SUS on pivoting](https://summify.io/discover/yc-sus-eric-migicovsky-dalton-caldwell-pivoting-pitching-msWFar); [YC Startup School, "All about pivoting"](https://chartable.com/podcasts/startup-school-by-y-combinator/episodes/40281738-all-about-pivoting-by-dalton-caldwell)
- **Startup Genome:** pivoting once or twice was associated with 3.6x better user growth and 52% less premature scaling than pivoting never or more than twice. **[Weak-Moderate]** — [Startupgeist](https://medium.com/startupgeist-articles/startup-success-why-startups-succeed-8bc397493a7b)
- **PG on narrowing (from memory, wording unverified):** it is worth asking whether there is a subset of the market where you can get a critical mass of users quickly. **[Strong source, wording unverified]** — [paulgraham.com/ds.html](https://paulgraham.com/ds.HTML)
- **Vohra:** narrow to the segment that would be "very disappointed" and build for them, rather than averaging across everyone. **[Strong]** — [Superhuman blog](https://blog.superhuman.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)
- **Andreessen:** do whatever is required, including rewriting the product or moving into a different market. **[Moderate; wording unverified]** — [Bokardo](https://bokardo.com/archives/origin-product-market-fit/)

### Inferences
- **Recommended decision logic for FollowUp** (synthesis):
  1. **Don't pivot yet.** About 2 months of thin data, gathered while the founder was mostly building, can't tell "wrong market" apart from "not enough selling." This is Caldwell's product-vs-sales distinction. A pivot now would be a reaction to too little information.
  2. **Narrow first** (change the Persona or Promise lever, not the product). For example: Toronto/GTA solo realtors who already get inbound leads from a specific source, with one promise ("every new lead gets a reply in minutes, and you see what was said"). Narrow is cheaper than a pivot and makes outreach and onboarding repeatable.
  3. **Set a dated decision gate,** for example 6-8 weeks of founder-led selling to the narrowed segment. Pass: 3-5 realtors with leads flowing weekly, at least 3 paying, and at least 1 real booking or saved deal traced to FollowUp. If it passes, persist and push for weekly growth. If it fails despite enough attempts (roughly 50-100+ targeted conversations, using the weak 50-150-conversations-per-10-customers estimate), treat it as a demand or problem signal and change the Problem or Persona lever. That is a real pivot, ideally toward something the founder knows well.
- Only one variable should change per cycle, so the founder can tell what caused the result.

### Gaps
- "Talk to 100 customers before building" and "10 paying customers in 90 days" are widely repeated, but this session traced neither to a primary named source. Don't quote them as YC or First Round rules.
- Lenny's "Art of the pivot" part 2 (how, why and when to pivot) is paywalled; its specific criteria weren't accessible.
- No published, evidence-based timeline that says when a pre-PMF B2B SMB startup should pivot. Every decision-gate number above is synthesis.

## 6. Is there specific advice for solo non-technical founders building with AI coding tools (the trap of building because it's easy)?

### Takeaway
The 2025-2026 consensus is that AI coding tools made building nearly free but did nothing for distribution, so founders who are uncomfortable selling now drift into building even faster. The consensus is real, but it is almost entirely practitioner opinion and anecdote; no rigorous data on outcomes for AI-built startups was found.

### Cited Findings
- **A mentor's view:** "Every founder I mentor knows they should talk to users, and vibe coding just made it easier to skip that part." AI removes the friction that used to force founders to pause and check with customers. **[Weak-Moderate: practitioner opinion]** — [MentorCruise, "The vibe coding trap"](https://mentorcruise.com/blog/the-vibe-coding-trap-why-founders-are-building-faster-and-understanding-less-77f0c/)
- **"Distribution is the new moat":** vibe coding speeds up building but not distribution. Durable advantage moves to buyer insight, distribution, retention and turning attention into trials. **[Weak: opinion]** — [Towards AI, "The $0 CAC matrix"](https://pub.towardsai.net/the-0-cac-matrix-why-vibe-coded-apps-need-a-distribution-machine-before-launch-1932c32b5cd1); [Towards AI, "Vibecoding was the easy part"](https://pub.towardsai.net/vibecoding-was-the-easy-part-880b6f6f4805)
- **Founder anecdote:** two solo products built and shipped in 48 hours, "the building part was genuinely easy," revenue was zero because nobody knew the products existed. Another post says founders uncomfortable with marketing retreat into adding features instead of finding users. **[Weak: anecdote]** — [DEV, "Vibe coding won't make you rich"](https://dev.to/solobillions/vibe-coding-wont-make-you-rich-distribution-will-2lh9); [DEV, "The builder trap"](https://dev.to/cypriantinasheaarons/the-builder-trap-why-more-building-is-keeping-you-broke-3ekg); [DEV, "Stop vibe coding, start getting customers"](https://dev.to/hunter_g_50e2ec233acd07b5/stop-vibe-coding-start-getting-customers-distribution-is-the-new-ai-moat-5hha)
- **YC's position:** YC's core advice is still to talk to users while building (Kraftful survey: 60% of YC founders do it daily). Garry Tan is publicly pro-AI-coding. No primary YC partner post was found that frames "AI makes building cheap, so selling matters more." **[Weak-Moderate]** — [Kraftful](https://www.kraftful.com/blogs/how-yc-founders-talk-to-users)
- **Unverified circulating claim:** that 25% of YC's Winter 2025 batch had codebases about 95% AI-generated. This comes from a secondhand blog, not a YC publication. **[Weak]** — [AI Hacks Substack](https://aihacks.substack.com/p/ycombinator-ai-vibe-coding-startups)
- **Rob Walling (pre-AI, but relevant to non-technical founders):** non-coders can build software products; validation comes first and others must share the problem and pay for a fix. **[Moderate]** — [Sproutworth interview with Rob Walling](https://www.sproutworth.com/create-your-first-software-product-cant-code/)
- **Solo context:** MicroConf cites a survey in which 50.7% of independent SaaS founders run their company solo. Being solo is normal, not a disadvantage in itself. **[Moderate]** — [MicroConf by-stage](https://microconf.com/by-stage)

### Inferences
- **FollowUp fits the AI-era trap exactly.** For a non-technical founder with an AI coding assistant, each polish, redesign or test-suite task gives fast, visible, satisfying progress. Selling to realtors gives slow, rejection-heavy, ambiguous progress. With no guardrail, the assistant makes the comfortable path the default. The design-brain, polish loops and admin tooling in this repo are good engineering hygiene pointed at the wrong bottleneck for this stage.
- **Practical guardrails** (synthesis, not sourced):
  1. Each week, write down the realtor conversations and onboardings before writing any code.
  2. Only accept AI-coding tasks that name a real user and the problem they hit.
  3. Use the AI assistant for go-to-market work (prospect lists, outreach drafts, call prep, follow-up sequences, onboarding scripts) as well as for code.
  4. Track one weekly number: businesses with real leads replied to through FollowUp.
- **Flip side:** cheap building is also an advantage. It makes "do things that don't scale" faster, for example building a custom integration overnight for the one realtor who asks. The trap is building for imagined users, not building in general.

### Gaps
- No rigorous 2023-2026 data on how AI-built or "vibe-coded" startups perform (activation, revenue, survival) compared with conventionally built ones. Every source found is opinion, anecdote or vendor content.
- No primary statement from YC, First Round, a16z or Lenny specifically addressing non-technical solo founders who build with AI coding tools.
